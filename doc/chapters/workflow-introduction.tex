\chapter{From a PD-LMI to Solver Constraints}
\label{sec:canonical-pdlmi}

\section{Begin With The Continuous Inequality}

Let the scheduling vector be
\(\boldsymbol\rho=(\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_\ell)\in\mathcal P\).  A
\emph{parameter-dependent LMI}\index{PD-LMI} is abbreviated \emph{PD-LMI} throughout this
manual and is the umbrella problem class named by the package.  When a model
also uses the bounded rate
\(\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}\in\mathcal V\) and derivatives of its decision
functions, we call it a \emph{differentiable parameter-dependent LMI}, or
\emph{DPD-LMI}\index{DPD-LMI}.  A canonical DPD-LMI is
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_i R_i(\boldsymbol\rho)
    \left(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}
      \frac{\partial x_i(\boldsymbol\rho)}{\partial\rho_j}\dot\rho_j\right)
  +F_0(\boldsymbol\rho)
  +\sum_i F_i(\boldsymbol\rho)x_i(\boldsymbol\rho)\prec0.
\end{equation*}
The known functions are the \(R_i\) and \(F_i\); the differentiable functions
\(x_i\) are decisions.  After known data are fixed, every term must remain
affine in the decision coefficients.  Removing the derivative term recovers
an ordinary PD-LMI.  Thus ``DPD-LMI'' adds a derivative qualifier; it does not
replace the package's PD-LMI name.

A concrete one-parameter example is the LPV Lyapunov condition
\begin{align}
  P(\rho)&\succeq I, \label{eq:canonical-positive}\\
  \dot P(\rho,\dot\rho)+P(\rho)A(\rho)
    +A(\rho)^{\mathsf T}P(\rho)&\preceq-\varepsilon I, \label{eq:lpv-spine}\\
  \dot P(\rho,\dot\rho)&=
    \frac{\partial P(\rho)}{\partial\rho}\dot\rho.\notag
\end{align}
Taking \(\dot\rho=0\), or removing \(\dot P\), gives the familiar PD-LMI
Lyapunov inequality.  In the worked path below \(P\) is \(2\)-by-\(2\),
\(\rho\in[0,1]\), \(\dot\rho\in[-1,1]\), and
\(\varepsilon=10^{-6}\).  The package inserts no hidden strictness margin;
the displayed \(\varepsilon I\) is part of the user's model.

\section{Bernstein Cells Support Continuous Lipschitz Matrix Functions}
\label{sec:bernstein-motivation}

In LPV analysis we often seek a Lyapunov matrix \(P(\boldsymbol\rho)\) that is
Lipschitz continuous on the compact parameter set \(\mathcal P\).  Gridding
\(\mathcal P\) makes the model finite and local, but independent cell
polynomials would not agree on a grid boundary.  The package instead shares
the complete Bernstein coefficient face between neighboring cells.  Because
the trace of a tensor-product Bernstein polynomial on a face depends only on
that face's coefficients, this sharing gives \(C^0\) continuity across every
cell boundary.  Each cell polynomial has bounded first derivatives, and a
continuous piecewise-polynomial function on a finite compact grid is therefore
globally Lipschitz.  Derivatives need not agree across a boundary: the
construction guarantees \(C^0\) continuity and Lipschitz regularity, not
\(C^1\) continuity.

On one normalized cell, the degree-\(m\) Bernstein
polynomial\index{Bernstein polynomial} basis is
\index{Bernstein basis}
\[
  B_i^m(\alpha)=\binom mi(1-\alpha)^{m-i}\alpha^i,
  \qquad i=0,\ldots,m,
\]
where \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) is the forward local
coordinate\index{local coordinate}.  The basis functions are nonnegative and
form a partition of unity\index{partition of unity}.  Therefore a matrix
polynomial \(C(\alpha)=\sum_iB_i^m(\alpha)C[i]\) lies in the convex hull of
its Bernstein coefficients\index{Bernstein coefficient}.  This
convex-hull property\index{convex-hull property} turns matrix-sign questions
into finite sufficient coefficient tests.  Exact degree
elevation\index{degree elevation} aligns operands without changing their
values, tensor-product bases\index{tensor-product basis} extend the same
reasoning to boxes, and separate cell-local storage handles nonuniform
physical partitions without inventing global samples.

The coefficients are not power-basis coefficients.  For example,
\[
  p(\alpha)=1+2\alpha+3\alpha^2
    =B_0^2(\alpha)+2B_1^2(\alpha)+6B_2^2(\alpha).
\]
Thus the degree\index{degree} is two and the Bernstein coefficient row is
\([1,2,6]\).  The basis sum remains one, so elevation can change this row
while preserving exactly the same polynomial.

The representation is local even when the physical domain is
two-dimensional.  The following degree-two matrix example has grid
\(\{[0,0.5,1],[0,1]\}\), hence two physical cells and nine local coefficient
matrices per cell.  The public traversal methods return physical-cell rows and
zero-based local labels; the accessor and the nested
\code{LocalValues}\index{LocalValues@\texttt{LocalValues}} storage path select
the same leaf.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpmatlab]
grid2 = {[0 .5 1], [0 1]};
A2 = pdmat(grid2, @(rho1,rho2) ...
    [1+rho1+rho2, rho1*rho2; ...
     rho1*rho2,   2+rho1^2], Degree=2);
physicalCells = A2.cells()
localLabels = A2.lbls()
secondCellCoefficients = A2.coeffs([2 1])
sameLeaf = A2.LocalValues{2}{1}
\end{lstlisting}

Both final variables are the flat nine-entry coefficient cell for physical
cell \([2,1]\); they are not a \(3\)-by-\(3\) grid of point samples.  The
remaining construction details, exact power-to-Bernstein conversion, and
tensor ordering are developed in Appendix~\ref{app:bernstein-math}.

\section{Separate Physical Nodes, Cells, And Local Labels}

For one parameter choose physical grid\index{physical grid} nodes
\index{physical node}
\[
  \rho_1^{(1)}<\rho_1^{(2)}<\rho_1^{(3)}.
\]
The subscript identifies the parameter direction, while the parenthesized
superscript identifies the one-based physical node.  The two physical
cells\index{physical cell/hypercube} have separate identities \(c=1\) and
\(c=2\):
\([\rho_1^{(1)},\rho_1^{(2)}]\) and
\([\rho_1^{(2)},\rho_1^{(3)}]\).  On cell \(c\), define the
forward coordinate
\[
  \alpha=\frac{\rho_1-\rho_1^{(c)}}
                   {\rho_1^{(c+1)}-\rho_1^{(c)}}\in[0,1].
\]
For local degree \(m=2\), the three coefficient labels are bracketed local
positions, not additional physical nodes:
\[
  C^{(c)}(\rho_1)=\sum_{i=0}^{2}B_i^2(\alpha)C^{(c)}[i].
\]

In MATLAB, the physical grid and degree are likewise independent choices.
The example below uses degree two.  Each cell has labels ([0]), ([1]), and
([2]), while adjacent cells share only their common face value.  The middle
coefficient of each cell is an interior control, not a physical-grid sample.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpmatlab]

physicalCells = M.cells()
localLabels = M.lbls()

The shared value \(20\) belongs to the physical interface
\(\rho_1^{(2)}\).  It is
seen as \(C^{(1)}[2]\) from the left cell and \(C^{(2)}[0]\) from the right.
This is \(C^0\) face sharing\index{continuity}: values meet, but the two cell-local derivatives
need not agree.  Consequently the representation is a continuous
piecewise-polynomial Bernstein representation on a gridding partition, not a
B-spline construction and not a claim of global differentiability.

\section{Form Matrix Algebra In Coefficient Space}

Fix a physical cell \(c\).  Its identity remains in the parenthesized
superscript while the bracketed integers are local Bernstein labels:
\(P=\sum_iB_i^mP^{(c)}[i]\) and
\(A=\sum_jB_j^nA^{(c)}[j]\).  Their ordered product is
\[
  PA=\sum_{k=0}^{m+n}B_k^{m+n}R^{(c)}[k],
  \qquad
  R^{(c)}[k]=\sum_{i+j=k}
    \frac{\binom mi\binom nj}{\binom{m+n}{k}}
    P^{(c)}[i]A^{(c)}[j].
\]
For the quadratic decision and affine known factor in this worked example,
write \(P_i=P^{(c)}[i]\) and \(A_j=A^{(c)}[j]\).  The four cubic
coefficients are
\[
  R^{(c)}[0]=P_0A_0,\qquad
  R^{(c)}[1]=\frac{P_0A_1+2P_1A_0}{3},
\]
\[
  R^{(c)}[2]=\frac{2P_1A_1+P_2A_0}{3},\qquad
  R^{(c)}[3]=P_2A_1.
\]
The pairs are first grouped by the anti-diagonal relation \(k=i+j\), then
combined with the binomial weights.  This is an ordered
convolution\index{ordered convolution}, not a
samplewise product.  Matrix order is preserved in every contribution.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpmatlab]
PA = P * A;
ATP = A' * P;
degreePA = PA.Degree
degreeATP = ATP.Degree
coefficientCountPA = numel(PA.coeffs(1))
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpoutput]

     3
degreeATP =
     3
coefficientCountPA =
     4
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Differentiate And Enumerate Rate Vertices}

For a degree-\(m\) cell \(c\) of width \(h_c\), differentiation is a forward
difference of adjacent local coefficients:
\[
  \frac{\partial P}{\partial\rho}
  =\frac{m}{h_c}\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}
    \bigl(P^{(c)}[i+1]-P^{(c)}[i]\bigr)B_i^{m-1}(\alpha).
\]
Because \(\dot P=(\partial P/\partial\rho)\dot\rho\) is affine in the rate,
checking both vertices \(-1\) and \(+1\) covers the whole interval
\(\dot\rho\in[-1,1]\).

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpmatlab]
D = rhodiff(P, [-1 1]);
derivativeDegree = D.Degree
isContinuous = D.IsContinuous
rateRowCount = size(D.coeffs(1),1)
coefficientsPerRow = size(D.coeffs(1),2)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpoutput]

  logical

     2

     2
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Compare The Residual And Choose A Certificate}\index{residual}

The following MATLAB expressions are a direct transcription of
\cref{eq:canonical-positive,eq:lpv-spine}.  On each cell, addition elevates the
degree-one derivative rows to the degree-three algebraic residual without
changing their represented values.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpmatlab]
residual = D + P * A + A' * P;
Cdecay = residual <= -1e-6 * eye(2);
Cpositive = P >= eye(2);
residualDegree = residual.Degree
decayConstraintCount = numel(Cdecay.Constraints)
positivityConstraintCount = numel(Cpositive.Constraints)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpoutput]

     3
decayConstraintCount =
    16
positivityConstraintCount =
     6
\end{lstlisting}

There are two physical cells, two rate vertices, and four degree-three local
labels, hence \(2\times2\times4=16\) decay constraints.  Positivity has no
rate rows and uses three degree-two labels on each cell, hence
\(2\times3=6\) direct constraints.  The shared face is deliberately visited
from each physical cell because direct certification is cell-local.

\section{Export The Finite Model To YALMIP}

\code{toYalmip} returns exactly the stored finite constraints.  It does not
select a solver, add an objective, or insert a margin.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpmatlab]
Fdecay = toYalmip(Cdecay);
Fpositive = Cpositive.toYalmip();
decayConstraintCount = length(Fdecay)
positivityConstraintCount = length(Fpositive)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpoutput]
decayConstraintCount =
    16
positivityConstraintCount =
     6
\end{lstlisting}

Use ordinary YALMIP calls after that boundary:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpmatlab]
solver = 'lmilab';
if exist('mosekopt', 'file') ~= 0
    solver = 'mosek';
end
opts = sdpsettings('solver', solver, 'verbose', 0);
sol = optimize([Fdecay, Fpositive], [], opts);
problemCode = sol.problem
\end{lstlisting}

The final code is produced by the installed solver and should be interpreted
with \code{sol.info}; it is not a package-owned status.  The complete tested
solver examples in \cref{sec:parameter-dependent-lyapunov-smoke,sec:solver-example}
include assertions appropriate to their solver paths.

In \(\ell\) dimensions, direction \(s\) has the one-based nodes
\(\mathcal G_s=\{\rho_s^{(1)},\ldots,\rho_s^{(k_s)}\}\).  The tensor grid is
\(\mathcal G=\mathcal G_1\times\cdots\times\mathcal G_\ell\), with
\(\prod_{s=1}^{\ell}k_s\) physical nodes.  A physical hypercube has the
separate one-based multi-index
\(\mathbf c=(c_1,\ldots,c_\ell)\), so there are
\(\prod_{s=1}^{\ell}(k_s-1)\) hypercubes.  With the forward coordinates
\[
  \alpha_s=\frac{\rho_s-\rho_s^{(c_s)}}
                  {\rho_s^{(c_s+1)}-\rho_s^{(c_s)}},
\]
the local degree-\(m\) tensor representation is
\[
  C^{(\mathbf c)}(\boldsymbol\rho)=
  \sum_{\mathbf i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}^{\ell}}
  \left(\prod_{s=1}^{\ell}B_{i_s}^{m}(\alpha_s)\right)
  C^{(\mathbf c)}[\mathbf i].
\]
The local Bernstein label \(\mathbf i\) is zero-based, while every
physical-node index and physical-cell component is one-based.  Earlier
dimensions vary more slowly in \code{cells},
\code{lbls}, \code{coeffs}, and rate-vertex traversal.  Cell-local spline and
gridding constructions for PD-LMIs motivate this architecture
\cite{masubuchi1998,xu2024}; Appendix~\ref{app:bernstein-math} develops the
underlying basis from degree-one interpolation to the general tensor case.
