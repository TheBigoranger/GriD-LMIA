\chapter[From a Parameter Box to Finite Constraints]{\texorpdfstring{From a Parameter Box to\\Finite Constraints}{From a Parameter Box to Finite Constraints}}
\label{sec:canonical-pdlmi}
\label{sec:bernstein-motivation}

This chapter begins with the LPV induced-\(L_2\)-gain problem, states the general DPD-LMI handled by the package, and then separates two examples on purpose. A two-dimensional nonuniform grid explains physical cells and tensor indexing without changing the mathematical domain. The Bernstein-basis section then opens with a one-dimensional, two-cell residual and carries that example through shared-face continuity and finite certificates. Detailed algebra remains in Appendices~\ref{app:bernstein-math} and~\ref{app:certificates}, while complete API contracts remain in Chapters~\ref{sec:pdmat}--\ref{sec:pdlmi}.

\section{Motivation}

Consider the LPV plant
\begin{align}
    \dot x(t)&=A(\boldsymbol\rho(t))x(t)+B_w(\boldsymbol\rho(t))w(t),\nonumber\\
    z(t)&=C_z(\boldsymbol\rho(t))x(t)+D_{zw}(\boldsymbol\rho(t))w(t),
\label{eq:lpv-l2-model}
\end{align}
where \(x\in\mathbb R^{n_x}\), \(w\in\mathbb R^{n_w}\), \(z\in\mathbb R^{n_z}\), and the measurable scheduling trajectory satisfies \((\boldsymbol\rho(t),\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}(t))\in\mathcal P\times\mathcal V\). For \(\gamma>0\), the zero-state induced-\(L_2\) objective is to certify
\[
    \lVert z\rVert_2<\gamma\lVert w\rVert_2
\]
for every nonzero admissible disturbance and every admissible scheduling trajectory.

A differentiable matrix \(P(\boldsymbol\rho)=P(\boldsymbol\rho)^{\mathsf T}\) supplies the scaled bounded-real DPD-LMI \cite{scherer1996}
\begin{align}
    P(\boldsymbol\rho)&\succ0,
\label{eq:canonical-positive}\\
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \dot P(\boldsymbol\rho,\dot{\boldsymbol\rho})+\operatorname{He}\!\left(P(\boldsymbol\rho)A(\boldsymbol\rho)\right) &
        P(\boldsymbol\rho)B_w(\boldsymbol\rho) &
        C_z(\boldsymbol\rho)^{\mathsf T}\\
        B_w(\boldsymbol\rho)^{\mathsf T}P(\boldsymbol\rho) &
        -\gamma I_{n_w} &
        D_{zw}(\boldsymbol\rho)^{\mathsf T}\\
        C_z(\boldsymbol\rho) &
        D_{zw}(\boldsymbol\rho) &
        -\gamma I_{n_z}
    \end{bmatrix}&\prec0
\label{eq:lpv-spine}
\end{align}
for every \((\boldsymbol\rho,\dot{\boldsymbol\rho})\in\mathcal P\times\mathcal V\), where \(\operatorname{He}(X)=X+X^{\mathsf T}\) and
\[
    \dot P(\boldsymbol\rho,\dot{\boldsymbol\rho})
    =\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}\dot\rho_s
    \frac{\partial P}{\partial\rho_s}(\boldsymbol\rho).
\]
The first inequality makes \(x^{\mathsf T}P(\boldsymbol\rho)x\) positive definite, and the block inequality supplies the dissipation condition for the gain bound. This application is one instance of the general derivative-dependent residual below. The strict signs are analysis notation: the package uses non-strict semidefinite comparisons and inserts no hidden numerical margin.

\section{General Problem and Assumptions}
\label{sec:general-problem-assumptions}

Let \(y=(y_1,\ldots,y_N)\) collect scalar parameter-dependent decision functions. The general task is to satisfy the single residual
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal F(\boldsymbol\rho,\dot{\boldsymbol\rho};y)
    ={}&F_0(\boldsymbol\rho)
    +\sum_{k=1}^{N}F_k(\boldsymbol\rho)y_k(\boldsymbol\rho)\\
    &+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{s=1}^{\ell}
    T_{k,s}(\boldsymbol\rho)\dot\rho_s
    \frac{\partial y_k}{\partial\rho_s}(\boldsymbol\rho)
    \preceq0,
    \qquad
    \forall(\boldsymbol\rho,\dot{\boldsymbol\rho})\in\mathcal P\times\mathcal V,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:general-pdlmi}
\end{equation}
The package fixes the abstract sets and decision class in \cref{eq:general-pdlmi} through the following assumptions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item The scheduling set and rate set are axis-aligned boxes; general polytopic or curved scheduling domains are outside the implemented model:
    \[
        \mathcal P=\prod_{s=1}^{\ell}[\underline\rho_s,\overline\rho_s],
        \qquad
        \mathcal V=\prod_{s=1}^{\ell}[\underline v_s,\overline v_s].
    \]
    \item The functions \(F_0\), \(F_k\), and \(T_{k,s}\) are known matrix functions of compatible dimensions, and the \(y_k\) are scalar decision functions. A matrix-valued decision is represented by vectorizing its independent scalar entries and assigning one component \(y_k\) to each entry, so symmetry can be encoded without treating a whole matrix as one scalar decision. Setting every \(T_{k,s}=0\) removes the derivative term and recovers an ordinary derivative-free PD-LMI.
    \item The theoretical statement uses differentiable \(y_k\). The package searches instead over continuous piecewise-Bernstein functions that are differentiable within each physical cell. Their values agree on shared faces, but their one-sided derivatives may differ; derivative data and the resulting residual conditions remain cell-local at those interfaces.
    \item Known data used by a certificate have cell-local polynomial coefficient evidence, and the assembled residual remains affine in the underlying YALMIP decisions. Supported numeric or known parameter-dependent factors may multiply a decision expression, but products of two decision-bearing expressions are outside the affine model.
    \item Dependence on \(\dot{\boldsymbol\rho}\) is affine. Consequently, checking the \(2^\ell\) vertices of \(\mathcal V\) is exactly equivalent to checking the complete rate box. This exact reduction is separate from both finite restrictions over the scheduling variables described next.
    \item MATLAB and YALMIP use non-strict semidefinite comparisons. A theoretical strict condition must therefore be modeled with an explicit user-selected margin such as \(S(\boldsymbol\rho)\succeq\varepsilon I\), and failure of a finite certificate is not proof that \cref{eq:general-pdlmi} is infeasible.
\end{itemize}

\section[Where the Finite Restrictions Enter]{\texorpdfstring{\hspace{0.3em}Where the Finite Restrictions Enter}{Where the Finite Restrictions Enter}}

There are two distinct restrictions over the scheduling continuum. First, gridding restricts the infinite-dimensional search over decision functions to a finite-dimensional space of continuous, cellwise-differentiable piecewise-Bernstein functions. Second, after a residual has been assembled in that space, Direct, P\'olya, Putinar, SparseFullBox, or FullBox imposes a finite sufficient certificate for its semidefinite sign throughout every cell. Either choice may be conservative, and changing the decision grid or degree is conceptually different from changing the certificate family or order.

The tensor grid itself is exact geometry: it partitions all of the assumed box \(\mathcal P\) into physical cells with disjoint interiors whose union is \(\mathcal P\); adjacent closed cells share their boundary faces. It is neither point sampling nor an approximation of the domain; gridding is an approximation method because it restricts the admissible decision functions on that exact domain. Likewise, the affine rate dependence in \cref{eq:general-pdlmi} is checked exactly at the vertices of \(\mathcal V\); rate-vertex traversal is not a third approximation layer.

\section[Gridding the Parameter Domain: a Separate Two-Dimensional Example]{\texorpdfstring{\hspace{0.3em}Gridding the Parameter Domain: a Separate Two-Dimensional Example}{Gridding the Parameter Domain: a Separate Two-Dimensional Example}}
\label{sec:intro-gridding}
\index{physical grid}\index{physical node}\index{physical cell}\index{tensor grid}

To make the tensor geometry visible, first isolate the grid and indexing geometry; the following Bernstein-basis section introduces the separate one-dimensional residual. Choose the nonuniform grids
\[
    G_1=\{0,0.4,1\},
    \qquad
    G_2=\{0,0.25,1\}.
\]
Their tensor product \(G=G_1\times G_2\) has nine physical nodes and exactly partitions \([0,1]^2\) into four physical cells. The one-based cell indices are \((1,1)\), \((1,2)\), \((2,1)\), and \((2,2)\).

For a tensor-product polynomial of degree two in each direction, every selected cell contains the nine zero-based local labels \(\mathbf i=(i_1,i_2)\in\{0,1,2\}^2\). Those labels identify Bernstein coefficients inside the cell; they are not nine additional physical sample points. In general, if direction \(s\) has \(k_s\) physical nodes, then \(G=G_1\times\cdots\times G_\ell\) has \(\prod_s k_s\) physical nodes and \(\prod_s(k_s-1)\) physical cells, while a degree-\(m\) tensor basis has \((m+1)^\ell\) local labels per cell. \Cref{sec:bernstein-tensor-grids} gives the complete traversal convention.

\section[Bernstein Basis on Each Cell]{\texorpdfstring{\hspace{0.3em}Bernstein Basis on Each Cell}{Bernstein Basis on Each Cell}}
\label{sec:continuous-bernstein-representation}
\index{Bernstein basis}\index{cell-local storage}\index{local Bernstein label}

The two-dimensional interlude established only the grid and indexing geometry. To introduce the cellwise Bernstein representation and the later certificate bookkeeping, now turn to a separate one-dimensional example with two cells and three degree-two coefficients per cell. Let
\begin{equation}
    S(\rho)=P(\rho)-H(\rho)\succeq\varepsilon I,
    \qquad 0\le\rho\le1,
\label{eq:running-residual}
\end{equation}
where \(P(\rho)\) is a continuous symmetric degree-two decision and
\[
    H(\rho)=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        1+\rho & 0.1\rho(1-\rho)\\
        0.1\rho(1-\rho) & 2+\rho^2
    \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Choose the physical grid \(G_1=\{0,0.5,1\}\), whose three nodes define the cells \(\mathcal H_1=[0,0.5]\) and \(\mathcal H_2=[0.5,1]\). The shared value \(0.5\) is one physical node and the boundary of both cells.

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpmatlab]
yalmip('clear')
grid = {[0 0.5 1]};
H = pdmat(grid, @(rho) ...
    [1 + rho, 0.1 * rho * (1 - rho); ...
     0.1 * rho * (1 - rho), 2 + rho^2], Degree=2);
P = pdvar(2, grid, "symmetric", Degree=2);

physicalCells = P.cells()
physicalCellCount = P.ncell()
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpoutput]
physicalCells = 2×1 double
     1
     2

physicalCellCount = 2
\end{lstlisting}

For cell \(c\), let \(a_c=\rho^{(c)}\), \(b_c=\rho^{(c+1)}\), and pull the physical coordinate back to
\[
    \alpha_c=\frac{\rho-a_c}{b_c-a_c}\in[0,1].
\]
The normalized degree-two Bernstein basis is
\[
    B_0^2(\alpha)=(1-\alpha)^2,\qquad
    B_1^2(\alpha)=2(1-\alpha)\alpha,\qquad
    B_2^2(\alpha)=\alpha^2.
\]
On each running-example cell,
\begin{equation}
    P^{(c)}(\rho)=\sum_{i=0}^{2}B_i^2(\alpha_c)P^{(c)}[i],
    \qquad c=1,2.
\label{eq:running-bernstein}
\end{equation}
The coefficient label \(i\) is zero-based and local to the already selected physical cell. The three matrices \(P^{(c)}[0]\), \(P^{(c)}[1]\), and \(P^{(c)}[2]\) are control coefficients, not samples at three additional physical grid points.

Because the basis functions are nonnegative and sum to one, \(P^{(c)}(\rho)\) is a convex combination of its three local coefficient matrices. The endpoint identities \(B_0^2(0)=1\) and \(B_2^2(1)=1\) expose the cell faces directly. A degree-two \code{pdvar} on two cells therefore has six local positions but only five global matrix controls:
\[
    P^{(1)}[2]=P^{(2)}[0].
\]
The package creates this equality by reusing the same YALMIP decision handle on both sides of the shared face; it does not append a later continuity constraint. The two interior controls \(P^{(1)}[1]\) and \(P^{(2)}[1]\) remain independent, so the represented function is continuous but need not have a continuous derivative at \(\rho=0.5\).

The same indexing appears through the inspection methods:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpmatlab]
localLabels = P.lbls()
cell1Coefficients = P.coeffs(1);
cell2Coefficients = P.coeffs(2);
localCount = numel(cell1Coefficients)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[style=dpoutput]
localLabels = 3×1 double
     0
     1
     2

localCount = 3
\end{lstlisting}

Known \(H\) and decision \(P\) use the same cell pullbacks and coefficient labels. Subtraction therefore forms the running residual \(S=P-H\) coefficient by coefficient, with exact degree elevation performed automatically if compatible operands have different degrees. \Cref{sec:bernstein-higher-degree,sec:bernstein-degree-elevation,sec:bernstein-product-math} develop the corresponding higher-degree, elevation, and multiplication rules.
